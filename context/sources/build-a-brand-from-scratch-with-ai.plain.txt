PROMPT 01 · WHY FINDER

You are a brand strategist who has read Simon Sinek's "Start With Why" twice and has zero patience
for corporate mush. Your job is to find the WHY behind my brand.
About me: I'm [your name]. I run (or I'm starting) [business or project] for [who it's for] in
[city, region or online].
How to run this:
- Ask me one question at a time and wait for my answer before the next one.
- Ask at least 12 questions. Mix the easy ones (why did you start this) with the uncomfortable ones
(what makes you angry about your industry, what would you keep doing if nobody paid you, what do you
say at a party when someone asks what you do and you can see their eyes glaze).
- When I give a vague answer like "I want to help people", push back. Ask for a specific moment, a
specific person, a specific day.
- Dig for the origin story: a frustration, a turning point, someone who did it badly and made me
think "I can do better".
When you have enough, give me:
1. My WHY in one sentence, in this format: "To [contribution] so that [impact]."
2. My HOW: 3 to 5 principles or behaviors that make me different from the others.
3. My WHAT: a plain-English description of what I sell, no jargon.
4. Three versions of a one-line belief I could put on my website, from safe to bold.
5. The single best story from my answers that proves the WHY, 120 words, first person, in my words.
Use my own words wherever you can. If anything I said sounds like it came off a motivational poster,
call it out and ask me again.

THE BIG ONE · DO THIS BEFORE ANYTHING ELSE

THE DISCOVERY
INTERVIEW
A real brand agency starts every job with a discovery session. It costs thousands because it takes hours and
somebody has to ask the awkward questions. Your AI will ask them for free and it doesn't get bored. Budget 30
to 45 minutes. Answer out loud with voice typing if you can. You'll say better stuff than you'd type.
PROMPT 02 · BRAND DISCOVERY INTERVIEW

Act as a senior brand strategist running a paid discovery session with a new client. I'm the client.
Interview me, then write my Brand Discovery Brief. This brief will be the foundation for my name,
logo, colors, fonts and voice, so do it properly.
My starting point: [business name or "no name yet"], [what I sell], [who buys it], [how long I've
been doing it], [what I charge, roughly].
Interview me in five rounds. One question at a time. Wait for each answer.
Round 1, the business: what I sell, what problem it solves, what a customer's life looks like before
and after, why someone would pick me over doing nothing.
Round 2, the people: my best customer ever (describe them), my worst customer ever, where my
customers hang out online and offline, what they're tired of hearing.
Round 3, the competition: who else they could buy from, what those brands get right, what they all
do that's boring or annoying.
Round 4, the personality: if my brand walked into a bar, who would it be; three words I want people
to use about me; three words I never want to hear; brands I love from any industry and why.
Round 5, the practical stuff: budget, deadlines, where the brand shows up first (Facebook, website,
truck wrap, packaging), anything I'm keeping (an old logo, a color I'm attached to).
Rules: if an answer is vague, ask a follow-up. If I contradict myself, point it out. Don't move to
the next round until you understand this one.
Then write the brief with these headings: The Business in One Paragraph, The Why (draft), Ideal
Customer, Competitors and the Sea of Sameness, Personality (3 traits, plus 3 anti-traits), Visual
Direction Notes, Must-Haves and Never-Evers, Open Questions. End with the 3 biggest risks to this
brand, stated bluntly.

Save the brief it gives you. You'll paste it into almost every prompt after this one.

ORIGINALITY IS OVERRATED

STEAL LIKE
AN ARTIST
“The secret to creativity is knowing how to hide your sources.”

Usually credited to Albert Einstein. He almost certainly never said it. Quote Investigator traces it to the
writer C. E. M. Joad in 1926 ("the height of originality is skill in concealing origins"), and the Einstein
version shows up in 1989, decades after he died. Source. Which is funny, because the quote hid its own source.

Whoever said it, the point stands. Every brand you admire is a remix of things its makers loved. Austin Kleon
wrote a whole book about this, Steal Like an Artist: nothing is completely original, so collect what moves you,
study it, mix it, and turn it into something that's yours. austinkleon.com/steal.

T HE HONEST CAVEAT

Most of what you'll read online about color psychology is overconfident. The research is mixed. Meaning
shifts with culture (white is weddings in much of the West and mourning in parts of East Asia), with shade
(navy and baby blue are different animals) and with category. What matters more than any chart: does the
color fit your personality, and does it stand out from your competitors? If every roofer in town is blue, the
orange roofer gets remembered.

60-30-10 · CONTRAST · HEX CODES

BUILD THE
PALETTE
The 60-30-10 rule
Borrowed from interior design. 60 percent is your dominant color (backgrounds, big areas). 30 percent is your
secondary (panels, type, supporting shapes). 10 percent is your accent, the thing you want eyes on: buttons,
one key word, the call to action. If everything is the accent, nothing is.

Contrast is not optional
If people can't read it, the brand doesn't exist. The accessibility standard (WCAG, level AA) asks for a contrast
ratio of at least 4.5:1 for normal text and 3:1 for large text, roughly 18 point and up or 14 point bold. Check every
text and background pair with the WebAIM contrast checker. The full rule is at w3.org.

TWO FONTS · ONE JOB EACH

TYPOGRAPHY

06

• Two fonts, maximum. One for headlines with personality, one for body text that disappears and lets people
read. A third font is how you end up looking like a yard sale flyer.
• Contrast, not conflict. Pair a loud headline with a calm body. Two loud fonts fight. Two calm fonts snooze.
• One family is a legit choice. Bold for headlines, regular for body. Done.
• Legibility first. Body text at 16 px or more on screens, line height around 1.4 to 1.6, lines of roughly 45 to 75
characters. Test on your phone in sunlight.
• Check the license. Google Fonts and Fontshare are free for commercial use. Paid fonts can have separate
terms for logos, apps and web.

• Find your taste. Fonts In Use shows real brands and the fonts they use. Fontjoy generates pairings to start from.

PROMPT 09 · FONT PAIRING

Recommend 5 headline and body font pairings for [brand], a [business type] whose personality is [3
traits] and whose archetype is [archetype]. Free fonts only, from Google Fonts or Fontshare, and

1. Headline font and body font, with the weights to use.
2. Why they work together (contrast, shared proportions, mood).
3. Sizes for web: H1, H2, body and caption in px, plus line height.

COMMON
MISTAKES
MISTAKE

THE FIX

Starting with the logo

The logo is the last 10 percent. Without the why, it's decoration.

Designing for yourself

You're not the customer. Your taste matters, their gut matters more.

Copying the category leader

You'll look like the cheaper version of them.

Too many colors and fonts

Restraint reads as confidence.

Trends

Gradients and blobs date fast. Make the decision you'll still like in five years.

Being nobody

A faceless brand has to outspend everyone. You can't. Show up as you.

Letting AI pick for you

AI gives you options. You make decisions. The whole value is in the deciding.

Rebranding every six months You get bored of your brand long before customers notice it. Consistency compounds.
Never asking real people

Five customer conversations beat fifty AI critiques.

Forgetting the boring stuff

Trademark search, domain, social handles, font licenses. Do it before the shirts.

O NE MORE TIP FROM THE PROS

Brand people judge a brand by its least polished touchpoint: the email signature, the invoice, the voicemail,
the reply to a one-star review. That's where the brand is real. Run those through your brand brain too.
